At the Edge of the Periodic Table

Investigating the reaction dynamics of superheavy-element synthesis at the limits of nuclear stability

How heavy can matter become? Where does the periodic table end? These fundamental questions motivate my research on the synthesis of heavy and superheavy elements.

My research investigates the reaction dynamics governing the formation of superheavy nuclei in heavy-ion collisions and the fundamental limits of nuclear stability. Through reaction modelling, uncertainty quantification and scientific computing, I develop predictive frameworks for experiments searching for new elements.

Research Overview

The formation of superheavy nuclei is governed by a competition between fusion, quasifission and fission. Only a tiny fraction of heavy-ion collisions result in a surviving superheavy nucleus. Understanding and predicting these rare events is the central goal of my research.

My research develops computational models of heavy-ion reactions to understand and predict the formation of heavy and superheavy nuclei. Stochastic simulation, uncertainty quantification and scientific computing provide the principal methods for this work.

The synthesis probability is determined by three competing processes: capture, compound-nucleus formation and survival against fission.

Research Questions

The central challenge

Superheavy-element synthesis lies at the intersection of nuclear structure, reaction dynamics and quantum many-body physics.

The challenge is to understand why some heavy-ion collisions produce a new nucleus while most result in quasifission or fission. Reliable predictions are essential for guiding experiments aimed at discovering elements beyond Z = 118 and exploring the predicted island of stability.

  • How heavy can matter become?
  • Where does the periodic table end?
  • How do touching nuclei evolve toward fusion rather than quasifission?
  • Can production cross sections for new elements be reliably predicted?
  • Do our theories remain predictive at the limits of nuclear stability?

Short answers from the literature

How heavy can matter become?

This is not yet known. The heaviest element made is oganesson (Z = 118); searches for elements 119 and 120 have found nothing so far. Models predict about 6,500 to 8,500 bound nuclei, with half-life predictions differing by up to 11 orders of magnitude (Smits et al. 2024, pp. 2–3, 19).

Where does the periodic table end?

It may have no fixed end. At Z ≈ 171 the 1s electron level enters the negative-energy continuum, which the authors argue requires new mathematics rather than ending the elements. Periodicity breaks down earlier: some elements beyond 120 have no clear place in the table (Smits et al. 2024, pp. 5, 20).

Do our theories remain predictive at the limits of nuclear stability?

Common approximations fail at the edge. Relativistic and quantum-electrodynamic effects grow strongly with Z, and nuclear predictions there are extrapolations, so the authors call for Bayesian uncertainty quantification (Smits et al. 2024, pp. 3–5).

Source: Smits et al. (2024), The quest for superheavy elements and the limit of the periodic table.

Superheavy-Element Synthesis

03

Uncertainty Quantification

Constraining reaction models using experimental data, statistical inference and sensitivity analysis to quantify predictive confidence and identify the most influential physical parameters.

04

Scientific Computing

Designing computational frameworks, simulation workflows and data-intensive methods for large-scale nuclear physics calculations, parameter estimation and model validation.

Research Contributions

  • Modelling fusion and quasifission dynamics in heavy-ion collisions
  • Calibration of reaction models against experimental data
  • Uncertainty-aware predictions of superheavy-element production cross sections
  • Development and application of KEWPIE-based reaction simulations
  • Computational frameworks for reaction dynamics and model validation

Why It Matters

Superheavy nuclei exist only because quantum shell effects counteract enormous Coulomb repulsion between protons.

Their study helps answer fundamental questions:

  • How heavy can matter become?
  • Where does the periodic table end?
  • Can long-lived superheavy nuclei exist?
  • How predictive are our theories at the limits of nuclear stability?

Research Vision

To develop predictive theories of nuclear reactions that guide the discovery of new elements and reveal the ultimate limits of the periodic table.

Current Projects

Current project

KEWPIE3 development and validation

Objective: validate and publish KEWPIE3, the refinement of the KEWPIE2 cascade code (which describes the survival of the excited nucleus) with an implemented and calibrated formation step, for fusion-evaporation cross-section predictions in superheavy element synthesis.

Key question: how can the calculation of the compound nucleus formation probability be improved with Langevin-based stochastic approaches?
