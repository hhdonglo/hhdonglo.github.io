Research Overview

How are heavy and superheavy nuclei formed?

My research develops computational models of heavy-ion reactions to understand and predict the formation of heavy and superheavy nuclei. Stochastic simulation, uncertainty quantification and scientific computing provide the principal methods for this work.

Research Background

My research is in theoretical nuclear physics, on the reaction mechanisms that lead to the formation of heavy and superheavy nuclei. My training began with a BSc in Physics at the University of Ghana, followed by the Erasmus Mundus Joint Master's in Nuclear Physics at universities in Spain, Italy and France, where I studied nuclear structure with the symmetry-conserving configuration mixing method.

My doctoral research at the University of Caen Normandy, within the CEA NUMERICS programme at GANIL, moved to reaction dynamics. It addressed the synthesis of superheavy elements by fusion–evaporation reactions, in particular the formation of the compound nucleus, which I implemented in the KEWPIE2 code as the basis of KEWPIE3.

Since then my work has combined reaction modelling with calibration against experimental data and uncertainty quantification, supported by scientific computing. The same methods carry over to machine learning and data engineering, which I develop alongside the physics.

Questions at the Edge of the Periodic Table

How heavy can matter get?

This is not yet known. The heaviest element made is oganesson (Z = 118); searches for elements 119 and 120 have found nothing so far. Models predict about 6,500 to 8,500 bound nuclei, with half-life predictions differing by up to 11 orders of magnitude (Smits et al. 2024, pp. 2–3, 19).

Where does the periodic table end?

It may have no fixed end. At Z ≈ 171 the 1s electron level enters the negative-energy continuum, which the authors argue requires new mathematics rather than ending the elements. Periodicity breaks down earlier: some elements beyond 120 have no clear place in the table (Smits et al. 2024, pp. 5, 20).

Do the laws of physics still hold at the edge?

Yes, but common approximations fail. Relativistic and quantum-electrodynamic effects grow strongly with Z, and nuclear predictions there are extrapolations, so the authors call for Bayesian uncertainty quantification (Smits et al. 2024, pp. 3–5).

Source: Smits et al. (2024), The quest for superheavy elements and the limit of the periodic table.

Research Questions

The central challenge

Superheavy-element synthesis lies at the intersection of nuclear structure, reaction dynamics and quantum many-body physics.

The challenge is to understand why some heavy-ion collisions produce a new nucleus while most result in quasifission or fission. Reliable predictions are essential for guiding experiments aimed at discovering elements beyond Z = 118 and exploring the predicted island of stability.

Research questions

  • How do touching nuclei evolve toward fusion rather than quasifission?
  • Can production cross sections for elements beyond Z = 118 be reliably predicted?
  • Which reaction mechanisms dominate the formation of superheavy nuclei?
  • How strongly do experimental data constrain model parameters and predictions?
  • What are the fundamental limits of nuclear existence and stability?

Why This Research Matters

The existence of superheavy nuclei is not guaranteed. Enormous Coulomb repulsion should drive very heavy nuclei apart, yet quantum shell effects provide additional stability that allows some of them to exist.

Understanding these systems addresses fundamental questions:

  • How heavy can matter become?
  • Where does the periodic table end?
  • What mechanisms govern the synthesis of new elements?
  • Can long-lived superheavy nuclei exist beyond those currently known?
  • How predictive are our theories at the limits of nuclear stability?

Approach

My research combines:

  • Nuclear reaction theory
  • Statistical modelling
  • Scientific computing
  • Data analysis
  • Uncertainty quantification

to build predictive frameworks for experiments aimed at discovering new elements and exploring the limits of nuclear existence.

Closing Statement

The formation of superheavy elements represents one of the most demanding problems in nuclear physics. By combining theoretical modelling, uncertainty-aware prediction and advanced computation, my goal is to improve our understanding of how new elements are created and how far the periodic table can ultimately extend.

Study of Reaction Mechanisms for the Synthesis of Superheavy Elements

PhD thesis, University of Caen Normandy (2024)

This thesis investigates the synthesis of super-heavy elements (Z ≥ 104) by fusion–evaporation reactions. These nuclei do not exist in nature because their macroscopic fission barriers vanish, and they are stabilised by quantum shell correction. The work improves the predictive power of the Kewpie2 model, with fusion–evaporation described in three stages (capture, formation and survival), by implementing the formation step in the code, the basis of KEWPIE3.

Key results
  • Formation step implemented in the Kewpie2 code for the first time, using both the overdamped and the full Langevin formalisms.
  • Injection-point distance optimised on cold and hot fusion datasets. Evaporation residue cross sections for hot fusion are reproduced, typically with accuracy better than an order of magnitude.
  • Cold fusion: multiple neutron emission channels are explained by an additional structural term. The 1n channel deviates from the data by a factor, while the 2n and 3n channels are within an order of magnitude.
  • Survival probability modelled with the latest data for superheavy elements. The formation and survival steps are tested extensively and compared with the Fusion-by-Diffusion model for sets of 27 cold and 24 hot fusion reactions.
  • Friction: analysis of the reduced friction coefficients in the overdamped approach suggests that the dynamics is not fully damped, so a full one-dimensional Langevin formalism is implemented and applied to hot fusion data. Model predictions are within an order of magnitude of the data.
  • Predictions for the synthesis of elements with Z = 119 and 120 align with results from other codes.
  • New method proposed for studying ratios of formation probabilities for the synthesis of 258No and 259Db.
  • Conclusion: the work makes the code a self-contained tool for studying superheavy-element synthesis and guiding future experiments.

Nuclear Structure Studies with Symmetry Conserving Configuration Mixing Methods

MSc dissertation, Universidad Autónoma de Madrid · Erasmus Mundus Joint Master in Nuclear Physics · September 2020 · Supervisor: Dr. Tomás Raúl Rodríguez Frutos

This dissertation describes the general framework of the symmetry conserving configuration mixing (SCCM) method, a beyond-mean-field approach based on the variational principle, and applies an axially symmetric version of it to study the structure of the even-even argon isotopes (A = 32 to 58). The same method is then used to explore the structure of 152,154Nd and 78Kr. The argon isotope study was carried out with T. R. Rodríguez Frutos and is available as a preprint on HAL.

Key results
  • Argon isotopes: they exhibit collectivity with mixing of oblate and prolate shapes.
  • Magic numbers N = 20 and 28 persist along the isotopic chain, shown by higher first excitation energies than in the neighbouring isotopes.
  • Neutron sub-shell closures (2p1/2 and pf-shell) at 56Ar and 58Ar are indicated by the first excitation energies and the nuclear charge radius.
  • Binding energy per nucleon and charge radii are reproduced along the isotopic chain, and the axial GCM-SCCM results show good agreement with the experimental data.
  • 152,154Nd are found to be well deformed, with two distinct rotational bands: prolate and oblate shapes coexist.
  • 78Kr shows an admixture of oblate and prolate shapes. The moment of inertia of the first excited 0+ state is about one third of that of the ground state, which reinforces the shape coexistence.
