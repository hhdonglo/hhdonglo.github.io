Research

Nuclear Reaction Dynamics and the Formation of Heavy and Superheavy Nuclei

How are heavy and superheavy nuclei formed, and how reliably can their production be predicted?

My research investigates the reaction mechanisms governing the formation of heavy and superheavy nuclei. I use stochastic reaction dynamics, computational modelling and uncertainty quantification to understand and improve predictions of fusion and evaporation processes.

Research Questions

The central challenge

Superheavy-element synthesis lies at the intersection of nuclear structure, reaction dynamics and quantum many-body physics.

The challenge is to understand why some heavy-ion collisions produce a new nucleus while most result in quasifission or fission. Reliable predictions are essential for guiding experiments aimed at discovering elements beyond Z = 118 and exploring the predicted island of stability.

  • How heavy can matter become?
  • Where does the periodic table end?
  • How do touching nuclei evolve toward fusion rather than quasifission?
  • Can production cross sections for new elements be reliably predicted?
  • Do our theories remain predictive at the limits of nuclear stability?

Short answers from the literature

How heavy can matter become?

This is not yet known. The heaviest element made is oganesson (Z = 118); searches for elements 119 and 120 have found nothing so far. Models predict about 6,500 to 8,500 bound nuclei, with half-life predictions differing by up to 11 orders of magnitude (Smits et al. 2024, pp. 2–3, 19).

Where does the periodic table end?

It may have no fixed end. At Z ≈ 171 the 1s electron level enters the negative-energy continuum, which the authors argue requires new mathematics rather than ending the elements. Periodicity breaks down earlier: some elements beyond 120 have no clear place in the table (Smits et al. 2024, pp. 5, 20).

Do our theories remain predictive at the limits of nuclear stability?

Common approximations fail at the edge. Relativistic and quantum-electrodynamic effects grow strongly with Z, and nuclear predictions there are extrapolations, so the authors call for Bayesian uncertainty quantification (Smits et al. 2024, pp. 3–5).

Source: Smits et al. (2024), The quest for superheavy elements and the limit of the periodic table.
