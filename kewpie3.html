KEWPIE3

A computational framework for fusion–evaporation reactions and superheavy-element synthesis.

Problem · Questions · Model · Framework · Calibration · Sensitivity · Uncertainty · Results · Outputs

Scientific Problem

Superheavy elements are made by fusing two heavy nuclei, and only a tiny fraction of collisions produce one: cross sections fall to picobarns and below. Experiments to reach new elements such as Z = 119 and Z = 120 depend on theoretical predictions to choose the reaction and the beam energy.

The weakest link in those predictions is the probability that the touching system forms a compound nucleus instead of re-separating. KEWPIE3 is a new version of the KEWPIE2 code built to address it with Langevin dynamics.

Research Questions

  • How can the compound-nucleus formation probability be calculated more reliably with Langevin-based stochastic approaches?
  • Which reaction and beam energy give the best chance of producing a new element?
  • How far can a predicted cross section be trusted, given the data used to calibrate the model?

Physical Model

Each reaction is calculated in three stages, and the evaporation-residue cross section is their product, summed over partial waves.

The three stages of the model
StageQuestionTreatment
CaptureDo the two nuclei get close enough to touch?Transmission through the Coulomb-plus-nuclear barrier
FormationDoes the touching system fuse, or re-separate by quasifission?Langevin (stochastic) dynamics from an injection point, with friction
SurvivalDoes the hot compound nucleus cool by emitting particles before it fissions?Statistical decay cascade: neutron, charged-particle and gamma emission against fission

Computational Framework

Physics engine

C++ modules for each stage, with shared level-density, mass-table and fission-barrier components. The loop over beam energies runs in parallel with OpenMP.

Workflow

One input file selects the reaction, the energies and the mode, and one command runs a forward calculation or a fit, then plots every neutron-emission channel. Python tooling reads the same input file as the C++ engine, so the two never disagree on settings.

Distributed runs

Sweeps over model settings, and single fits, can be distributed across several hosts with MPI. Results are collected into one table for comparison.

Calibration

The formation stage has two parameters that theory does not fix: the injection-point distance and the friction strength. They are fitted jointly to measured cross sections for all hot-fusion or all cold-fusion reactions.

Two optimisers are available: a random search refined by Newton–Raphson, which yields the Hessian and one-sigma errors, and Bayesian optimisation with a Gaussian-process surrogate of the loss surface. An error-weighted logarithmic chi-square gives statistically calibrated uncertainties.

Sensitivity Analysis

The fit is repeated across a grid of model choices outside the fitted parameters: the level-density model, the damping of shell effects, the friction in the fission width, the Kramers factor with collective enhancement, and a temperature correction to the fission barrier. Each setting is judged both by fit quality and by how stable the fit stays when the other choices change.

Uncertainty Quantification

The confidence region of the fit is traced by direct evaluation of the Δχ² surface, not by a Gaussian approximation, and refined along the correlation direction of the two parameters. Forward calculations at points spanning that region give a confidence corridor around every predicted excitation function.

Results

Validation against measured hot- and cold-fusion cross sections is in progress. Results will be reported in the KEWPIE3 publication and the manuscript on uncertainty analysis of nuclear reaction models, both in preparation.
