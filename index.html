Overview

Scope, objective and weekly structure

Each week is taught in three contacts. Every lecture provides a slide deck, a tutorial sheet and a supplementary note. All files are PDF. Select the deck preview or a button on a card to open it. Each lecture also has twenty multiple-choice practice questions that run in the browser, for practice and not assessment.

Scope

Mechanics: lectures 1 to 8. Thermal physics: lectures 9 to 12.

Objective

To prepare students to succeed in the PHYS 143 examination through conceptual understanding, technical competence and application to unfamiliar problems.

Weekly routine: attend all three contacts, attempt the tutorial before the answers, review the supplementary note, and use the deck checklist to find gaps.

Contacts per week

  1. Contact 1: build the physical idea.
  2. Contact 2: develop the model and method.
  3. Contact 3: apply, integrate and transfer.

Lecture 4

Work and kinetic energy

When a force does work, and how that work changes the speed of an object.

  1. 1Identify
  2. 2Quantify
  3. 3Extend
Learning outcomes
  • Calculate the work done by a constant force, and the total work done on an object.
  • Interpret the sign of work and the meaning of kinetic energy.
  • Apply the work–energy theorem to find speeds, distances and forces.
  • Calculate the work done by varying forces, including springs, and relate work to power.

Lecture 5

Potential energy and energy conservation

How stored energy and conservation of energy solve problems that forces alone make difficult.

  1. 1Identify
  2. 2Quantify
  3. 3Extend
Learning outcomes
  • Calculate gravitational and elastic potential energy, and say when a force has a potential energy.
  • Apply conservation of mechanical energy to find speeds and heights.
  • Include friction and other forces in an energy equation, and say where the energy goes.
  • Find a force from a potential energy, and read an energy diagram.

Lecture 7

Impulse and momentum

How impulse changes momentum, and how conservation of momentum solves collision problems.

  1. 1Define
  2. 2Conserve
  3. 3Extend
Learning outcomes
  • Calculate momentum and impulse, and relate them with the impulse–momentum theorem.
  • Apply conservation of momentum to isolated systems.
  • Solve one- and two-dimensional collision problems, and classify collisions as elastic or inelastic.
  • Use the coefficient of restitution and combine collisions with energy methods.

Lecture 8

Rotation

How torque, moment of inertia and angular momentum extend Newton's laws to rotating bodies.

  1. 1Describe
  2. 2Model
  3. 3Apply
Learning outcomes
  • Describe rotation using angular position, velocity and acceleration, and relate them to the linear motion of a point on the body.
  • Calculate moments of inertia and torques, and use Στ = Iα to find angular accelerations.
  • Use work, power and energy methods for rotating and rolling bodies.
  • Use angular momentum and its conservation, including when the moment of inertia changes.

Thermal physics

Lectures 9 to 12

Lecture 9

Temperature and heat

What temperature and heat mean, how they are measured, and how heat moves between bodies.

  1. 1Define
  2. 2Model
  3. 3Apply
Learning outcomes
  • Distinguish temperature from heat, and explain thermal equilibrium and the zeroth law.
  • Convert between the Celsius, Fahrenheit and Kelvin scales and describe how thermometers work.
  • Calculate thermal expansion and the heat needed to change a temperature, including simple calorimetry.
  • Calculate the rate of heat transfer by conduction and radiation, and describe convection.

Lecture 10

Thermal properties of matter

How the ideal-gas equation, phase changes and kinetic theory describe matter at the molecular level.

  1. 1Describe
  2. 2Represent
  3. 3Explain
Learning outcomes
  • Use the ideal-gas equation and the gas laws to calculate and compare the states of a gas.
  • Read pV and phase diagrams, and calculate the heat in a phase change, including calorimetry.
  • State the assumptions of the kinetic theory and explain pressure and temperature in terms of molecular motion.
  • Calculate the average kinetic energy and the rms speed of molecules, and select the right model in an unfamiliar problem.

Lecture 11

First law of thermodynamics

How energy is exchanged as heat and work, and how the first law describes each gas process.

  1. 1Represent
  2. 2Quantify
  3. 3Extend and apply
Learning outcomes
  • Describe a thermodynamic system, state and process, and calculate the work done by a gas from a pV diagram.
  • Apply the first law, ΔU = Q − W, to a process and to a cycle.
  • Calculate Q, W and ΔU for isochoric, isobaric, isothermal and adiabatic processes of an ideal gas.
  • Analyse a multi-step process or cycle in an unfamiliar problem, choosing the physics without being told.

Lecture 12

Second law of thermodynamics

Why processes run in one direction, what entropy measures, and the best efficiency an engine can reach.

  1. 1Direction
  2. 2Convert
  3. 3Limits
Learning outcomes
  • Distinguish reversible from irreversible processes, and calculate entropy changes to decide whether a process is allowed.
  • Analyse a heat engine or a refrigerator with an energy-flow diagram, including from a pV cycle.
  • State the second law and use the Carnot cycle to find the maximum efficiency and to test a claimed device.
  • Analyse an unfamiliar engine, refrigerator or entropy problem, choosing the physics without being told.

Expected skills

Physicist's Toolkits

  • Vector and component methods
  • Free-body diagrams
  • Graph interpretation
  • Estimation and dimensional analysis
  • Limiting cases
  • Conservation laws
  • Checking answers

Problem-solving framework

Identify, Set Up, Execute, Evaluate.

Further skills

  • Switching between representations: situation, diagram, graph, words, equation and number
  • Calculating with units and checking sign, magnitude and direction

Applications

Examples drawn from the lecture decks include the following.

  • Projectile motion
  • Vehicle safety and collisions: airbags and crumple zones
  • Rotating machinery: the flywheel
  • Thermal expansion of structures: gaps in railway rails
  • Internal-combustion engines and diesel compression
  • Heat engines and refrigerators
